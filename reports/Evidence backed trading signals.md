# Slow trend rules outlast fast retail signals

The best-supported way to time gold, US oil and bitcoin is a **slow trend filter**: hold the asset while its price is above a long moving average or its past return is positive, and step aside otherwise. Almost nothing else has published evidence that holds up after trading costs and on data the researchers did not tune on. Even the trend evidence has limits. For gold, only monthly rules that look back two to four years passed a data-snooping test, and only just. For oil, the evidence comes from portfolios of many commodities, not from crude on its own. For bitcoin, one study reports results after fees: a published ensemble of breakout rules with about **18% net annual growth and a Sharpe ratio above 1.5** (2015 to March 2025). A widely quoted 2025 gold paper with a "Sharpe 2.88 / 43% a year" headline falls apart when its own tables are read closely. Its own cost test shows a Sharpe of about 0.94, which drops to roughly zero at 1.5 times its very low costs. Fast trend signals (days to weeks) have essentially stopped working on stock indices and currencies since about 2009, but have held up on commodities. Popular retail products have no independent evidence of an edge: paid Telegram or app "AI" signals, TradingView indicators, and grid, martingale or averaging-down bots. Regulators found that **74–89% of retail CFD accounts lose money**. The practical upshot for a backtest from 2025-01-01 is to test a short, pre-chosen list of published rules with their published settings, at one and a half to two times realistic costs. Treat the result as a consistency check, not proof. Under two years of data cannot statistically confirm any trading edge.

## Published edges shrink by half, so the bar for "works" is high

Some terms first. A **backtest** replays a rule on past prices. **Out of sample** means testing on data the rule was not designed or tuned on. The **Sharpe ratio** is return divided by volatility: about 0.5 is decent for one market, and 1 or more is rare and suspicious. **Data snooping** means trying many rule variants and reporting the winner. That produces impressive results by luck alone.

The research on research is sobering. Across 97 published stock-market predictors, returns were **26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication** ([McLean & Pontiff](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf)). Of 452 published anomalies, **65% failed a basic significance test and 82% failed a stricter multiple-testing threshold** when replicated ([Hou, Xue & Zhang](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v33y2020i5p2019-2133..html)). Harvey, Liu and Zhu argue that new findings should clear a t-statistic of 3.0 rather than the usual 2.0, and conclude that "most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false" ([NBER](https://nber.org/papers/w20592)). One counterpoint exists. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen report that about 82% of factors replicate under a different statistical approach, though that figure reached us through a secondary summary ([Systematically Biased](https://systematicallybiased.substack.com/p/pockets-of-replicability-post5)).

Technical trading rules fare worse than factors. A test of about 7,846 rules on a century of Dow data found that the best rule's apparent edge on major US indices disappeared after the mid-1980s ([Sullivan, Timmermann & White](https://bashtage.github.io/kevinsheppard.com/files/teaching/mfe/advanced-econometrics/Sullivan_Timmermann_White.pdf)). A later study found that the in-sample profits were "completely offset by low transaction costs". It also found that nobody could have picked the future best rule in advance, because rule performance does not persist ([Bajgrowicz & Scaillet](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:106:y:2012:i:3:p:473-491)). Bailey and co-authors show that with five years of daily data, trying more than about **45 rule variants almost guarantees one with an in-sample Sharpe of 1 and an expected real-world Sharpe of 0** ([CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/insidiousness-of-overfitting-investment-strategies-via-iterative-backtests)). These results set the standard for everything below. A rule earns a place on the list only if it was tested beyond its own discovery sample, ideally with costs, and ideally by someone other than the people selling it.

## Gold: only slow monthly trend rules cleared a data-snooping test

The most rigorous gold timing study is by Baur and co-authors (published in *International Review of Financial Analysis*, 2020). It tested more than 4,000 monthly long-gold-or-cash rules over **January 1990 to December 2015**: 18 technical, 15 fundamental and 4,095 seasonal combinations. The technical rules were moving-average crossovers and positive-past-return ("time-series momentum") rules. The winners were all very slow. **Holding gold when its 24-month return was positive (MOM24) earned 0.611% a month, versus 0.313% for buy-and-hold. Holding when price was above its 36-month moving average earned 0.607%, and above its 48-month average 0.600%** ([Quantpedia, Table 3](https://quantpedia.com/an-extensive-test-of-market-timing-strategies-in-the-gold-market/)). The paper then corrected for having tried many rules, using Hansen's SPA test. The best technical rule's corrected p-value was **0.079 on bullion and 0.066 on COMEX futures**, just above the conventional 0.05 cut-off. A step-wise version of the test identified three technical rules that outperformed. Seasonal rules (p ≈ 0.78–0.82) and fundamental/macro rules (p ≈ 0.24–0.27) failed clearly ([Quantpedia, Table 4](https://quantpedia.com/an-extensive-test-of-market-timing-strategies-in-the-gold-market/)). The three survivors are probably MOM24 and the 36- and 48-month moving-average rules, the top three in the returns table, but the paper's table does not name them. The authors say the edge comes from **stepping out of gold after a prolonged run of negative returns**, not from beating gold in bull markets. The published journal abstract is more negative still: results "are not robust to data-snooping biases" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057521918306227)). The Quantpedia page does not state the transaction costs.

The widely circulated "Forecast-to-Fill" paper (arXiv 2511.08571, November 2025, not peer reviewed) looks far stronger on paper but should be read as a rule template, not as evidence ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.08571)). It tests COMEX gold futures in six-month walk-forward windows from January 2015 to October 2025. It goes long when a blended score, 60% from a smoothed trend slope and 40% from a 50-day momentum flag (price above its level 50 days ago), is at least 0.52 and the trend is rising. It exits on the first of four conditions: a stop 2×ATR(14) below entry, a trailing stop 1.5×ATR(14) below the running peak, 30 trading days elapsed, or a bear score above 0.50. ATR is the average daily price range. Its headline **Sharpe of 2.88 comes from a strategy with 0.91% annual volatility and 2.65% annual growth**. The "43% a year" figure is that result scaled up on paper to 15% volatility, not a traded result. Three further problems undercut the headline. The paper's own cost-stress table shows **Sharpe 0.94 at its baseline costs, about 0 at 1.5× costs and −1.0 at 2× costs**, even though the baseline is only 0.7 basis points per round trip. Its appendix says trades fill at the next day's close, yet the headline uses same-day fills; the next-day result is Sharpe 2.28. And it never states the smoothing parameter for the trend leg, so the rule cannot be reproduced exactly. Retail gold CFD spreads are far wider than 0.7 basis points, so this edge would very likely vanish for a retail trader.

The faster ideas are weaker. Gold's **overnight-positive, day-session-negative pattern** was significant in COMEX futures from 1985 to 2012, and it also appeared in GLD, London fix prices and gold-related funds. The authors themselves note it "has weakened substantially" ([Blose & Gondhalekar](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/apeclt/v21y2014i18p1269-1272.html); [Blose, Gondhalekar & Kort](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s12197-017-9403-0)). A practitioner test of buying GLD at the close and selling at the next open found the effect "probably too small to trade by itself" ([QuantifiedStrategies](https://quantifiedstrategies.substack.com/p/a-quantitative-look-at-the-gold-overnight)). The famous **September/November "autumn effect"** (1980–2010) ([Baur 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/riibaf/v27y2013i1p1-11.html)) could not be confirmed in later data ([Borowski & Łukasik via reference-global](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/ijme-2024-0011.pdf)). A 2024 paper reports it has "been reversed and replaced by the winter effect" ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/vrs/ijomae/v60y2024i3p157-172n1001.html)). The only peer-reviewed high-frequency study, of 5-minute gold data from 2008 to 2014, searched 66,297 moving-average crossovers. It found that the **popular (1,50) through (1,200) 5-minute settings lost money** ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/high-frequency-technical-trading-of-gold-and-silver/)). Real yields used to be the strongest macro driver of gold, but the relationship broke down from autumn 2022 as central banks bought heavily ([Goldmoney](https://www.goldmoney.com/research/have-central-banks-lost-control-over-the-gold-price); [RBC WM](https://www.rbcwealthmanagement.com/en-us/insights/golds-regime-change)). RSI and Bollinger-band "oversold" buys on GLD show average 20-day gains of about 1.0–1.3% in an anonymous blog's tests with no costs ([reigraph](https://reigraph-blog.pages.dev/do-technical-indicators-actually-work)). That is close to what gold's normal upward drift would deliver from a random entry anyway.

## US oil: trend works across commodity portfolios, and the sharpest oil-specific rule is intraday

No study found tests a trend rule on crude oil alone, after costs and out of sample. The supporting evidence comes from portfolios of many commodities. Szakmary, Shen and Sharma tested monthly dual moving-average and channel-breakout rules on 28 commodity markets over 48 years. They found **positive returns after transaction costs in at least 22 of the 28 markets**, robust to data-mining adjustments, but the abstract does not break out crude oil ([RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbfina:v:34:y:2010:i:2:p:409-426)). Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen report trend following profitable across commodity futures back to 1880, net of estimated costs ([PDF](https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/hurst.pdf)). Its exact construction could not be checked against the paper. That construction is an equal blend of 1-, 3- and 12-month past-return signals, each scaled for volatility, so treat it as a commonly cited specification rather than a verified one. The strongest challenge comes from Huang, Li, Wang and Zhou (2020). Across 55 futures markets from 1985 to 2015, only 8 showed predictability even in sample. A rule based simply on each market's average drift did about as well as trend following ([RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:135:y:2020:i:3:p:774-794)).

The most useful recent finding for oil is good news by comparison. Kurth, Eisler, Rej and Bouchaud (July 2026) studied about 100 futures. The Sharpe ratio of the fastest trend signal fell from **0.84 (1995–2009) to 0.12 (2009–2025)**, and the slowest from 0.70 to 0.40. That decay sat in "small-tick" markets (stock indices and currencies). The **commodity sector showed "no appreciable degradation"**, and crude and Brent are in the paper's universe ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.01550)). A "small-tick" market is one where the minimum price increment is small relative to how much the price moves. The paper's tables do not list each contract's tick tier, so crude's exact classification is inferred from the sector result. The authors also showed the decay is in the signal itself, not in costs: even zero-delay, zero-cost execution was flat after 2008. On a single market, simple breakouts are thin. A practitioner backtest of price-channel rules on crude, heating oil, natural gas and gasoline futures found the raw edge "clearly insufficient to cover the commission costs and slippage" ([Benzinga](https://www.benzinga.com/general/education/25/07/46280812/price-channel-strategy-for-trend-following-energy-futures-backtest-and-optimization)). Live trend funds also struggled at times in 2025. A trend index was down 18.6% over the 12 months to April 2025, and AQR blamed commodity trend reversals for losses in September 2025 ([Hedgeweek](https://www.hedgeweek.com/aqr-outpaces-trend-following-peers-with-double-digit-h1-gains/); [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/aqrs-big-year-funds-climb-stock-picks-and-macro-moves)).

The best oil-specific rule is **intraday momentum**. Using the USO oil fund from 2006 to 2018, Wen and co-authors found that the return in the first half-hour of the session predicts the return in the last half-hour, and that a timing strategy built on this earns "substantial" profits ([RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:ecmode:v:95:y:2021:i:c:p:374-384)). On EIA inventory-report days, the half-hour containing the release becomes the predictor instead ([Energy Journal summary](https://www.iaee.org/ej/ejexec/ej44-5-Xu-exsum.pdf)). Inventory surprises move prices in the expected direction: a build pushes prices down and a draw pushes them up ([Ye & Karali](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:eneeco:v:59:y:2016:i:c:p:349-364)). But the effect has **no persistence beyond announcement day** ([EIA slides](https://www.eia.gov/finance/markets/reports_presentations/2017/yang.pdf)). It is therefore a same-day event, not a swing-trade signal, and trading the surprise itself needs analyst-consensus data. Timing matters for a backtest. The report comes out at **10:30 a.m. ET on Wednesdays, but holiday weeks usually move it to Thursday at 12:00 p.m. ET**. In 2025 there were seven such shifts, including 23 January, 29 May, 4 September and 13 November. The Christmas 2025 report came out on **Monday 29 December at 5:00 p.m. ET** ([EIA schedule](https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/supply/weekly/schedule.php)). A rule hard-coded to "Wednesday 10:30" would trade on empty bars in those weeks. Among event rules, only **production-cut** announcements at OPEC meetings moved prices significantly (1983–2008), and going long the day after the conference was profitable in sample ([Demirer & Kutan](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eneeco/v32y2010i6p1467-1476.html)). The IMF finds OPEC meeting effects of about 3% that do not last ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2022/english/wpiea2022183-print-pdf.pdf.md)). The "low Monday" effect is not statistically significant ([Auer 2014](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:eneeco:v:43:y:2014:i:c:p:82-88)), and no published short-term mean-reversion rule for crude was found.

## Bitcoin and ETH: one trend ensemble reports results after fees

Crypto has the only after-fee, out-of-sample trend result in this review. Zarattini, Pagani and Barbon ("Catching Crypto Trends", 2025) run each of **nine lookbacks: 5, 10, 20, 30, 60, 90, 150, 250 and 360 days**. For each lookback, a position opens when the daily close rises above that lookback's highest close. It exits on a trailing stop that each day moves to the higher of the previous stop and the midpoint between that lookback's highest and lowest close. The nine signals are averaged with equal weights. Each coin is sized to 25% target volatility, using 90-day volatility, with leverage capped at 200%. The universe is the 20 most liquid coins that have been listed for at least a year and trade at least $2M a day. Over January 2015 to mid-March 2025, at 0.10% costs, the authors report **about 18% net annual growth, about 11% annual alpha over bitcoin, and a net Sharpe above 1.5** ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/crypto-asset-trend-following-strategies/); [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/chf/rpseri/rp2580.html)). These details come from a paraphrase and the abstract, because the full paper could not be reached. Results at the paper's higher cost levels (0.25% and 0.50%) and for bitcoin alone were not visible. The authors' firm also sells research.

Simpler moving-average filters have peer-reviewed support. Detzel and co-authors (*Financial Management*, 2021) found that the ratio of bitcoin's price to its **5-, 10-, 20-, 50- and 100-day moving averages** predicts daily returns both in and out of sample ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:finmgt:v:50:y:2021:i:1:p:107-137)). Grayscale's version holds bitcoin while it is above its 50-day average and cash below. Since 2012 it beat buy-and-hold with lower volatility, but it **excludes fees and has no out-of-sample split** ([Grayscale](https://research.grayscale.com/reports/the-trend-is-your-friend-managing-bitcoins-volatility-with-momentum-signals)). A cautionary 2024 study found technical and sentiment measures generally poor at predicting bitcoin out of sample ([Shelton, JRFM](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jjrfmx/v17y2024i10p443-d1490211.html)). A 2026 thesis found that only 4 of 67 in-sample-significant variables consistently beat a random walk, though the survivors were mostly technical ([UDP thesis](https://doctorados.udp.cl/cms/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Tesis_Doctoral_Aliro_Sanhueza.pdf)).

Two Quantpedia rules need correcting against their primary pages. The **MAX rule** says: if bitcoin closes at its highest level of the last N days, hold it for the next day, and re-check every day. It is not a "buy the breakout and trail a stop" rule. Over 2015 to February 2022, the combined MAX-or-MIN version at 10 days returned 98.43% a year with a −37.67% maximum drawdown. Bitcoin itself had a −83.65% drawdown over the same period ([Quantpedia 2022](https://quantpedia.com/trend-following-and-mean-reversion-in-bitcoin/)). In the true out-of-sample window, **4 February 2022 to 20 August 2024, MAX kept working, and the 20-day MAX beat the 10-day**. The buy-at-the-N-day-low MIN leg gave "low or even negative returns" ([Quantpedia 2024](https://quantpedia.com/revisiting-trend-following-and-mean-reversion-strategies-in-bitcoin/)). The **intraday-seasonality rule** buys bitcoin at **21:00 UTC and sells at 23:00 UTC** every day. It returned 33% a year with a −22.45% drawdown over 2015 to February 2022 ([Quantpedia 2022](https://quantpedia.com/are-there-seasonal-intraday-or-overnight-anomalies-in-bitcoin/)) and 40.64% a year through June 2023, with a "rough period in 2022 and 2023". Restricting it to high-volatility days gave 37.26% a year with a −18.87% drawdown ([Quantpedia 2023](https://quantpedia.com/the-seasonality-of-bitcoin/)). Quantpedia's own strategy page instead says to enter at 22:00 and lists a −34.04% drawdown and "Transaction costs: Not Reported" ([Quantpedia strategy page](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/intraday-seasonality-in-bitcoin)). Neither rule reports costs. A daily round trip at 5 basis points a side costs roughly 36% a year, about as much as the gross return.

Derivatives data supports a risk filter, not an entry signal. The BIS "Crypto carry" paper (WP 1087, March/April 2023; a claimed 2025 revision could not be confirmed) covers April 2019 to January 2022. Over that period the annualised gap between futures and spot prices averaged about 10% a year and peaked near 60%. Higher carry predicted **lower prices at expiry, more negative skew (crash risk), higher volatility, and more short liquidations**: a 10% rise in carry predicted a 44% rise in short-futures liquidations the next month. The paper gives **no trading threshold**, and its cash-and-carry harvest had a Sharpe of about 0.6 before costs ([BIS PDF](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-1087-crypto-carry.pdf)). On-chain valuation metrics such as MVRV have no peer-reviewed out-of-sample support. Their "top above 3.7, bottom below 1.0" thresholds were picked after the fact from a handful of cycles ([Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/bitcoin-runs-on-a-clock-why-every-price-indicator-dies-and-the-halving-clock-doesnt/)). ETF flows mostly follow price ([Cointelegraph/DWF Labs](https://cointelegraph.com/news/bitcoin-etf-flows-follow-price-trends-dwf-labs?rand=50490)). For ETH, there is no ETH-only rule study. ETH is covered by Liu and Tsyvinski's finding of daily and weekly time-series momentum in BTC, ETH and XRP (gross of costs, about 2011–2018) ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24877/w24877.pdf)), and it is implicitly in the Zarattini top-20 universe. The sensible approach is to run bitcoin's rules on ETH with identical settings.

## Stock indices and the dollar: fast trend has vanished, but slow trend and a few calendar rules remain

For US indices, the classic slow rule is Faber's. On the last trading day of each month, hold the index if its close is above its 10-month simple moving average, otherwise hold cash ([quantstrat](https://rdrr.io/github/braverock/quantstrat/man/stratFaber.html)). It is not fragile: 8-, 10-, 12- and 14-month averages behave similarly ([CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/momentum-investing/are-momentum-strategies-fragile/)). Its often-quoted post-publication Sharpe improvement "from 0.45 to 0.80" comes only from a secondary summary and could not be checked against Faber's 2018 paper ([Swedroe/ETF.com](https://www.etf.com/sections/index-investor-corner/swedroe-why-financial-trends-persist)). The Bouchaud-group paper corrects an earlier assumption in these notes: stock indices sit in the small-tick tier, and **index trend "has effectively vanished"** after 2009 at fast speeds ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.01550)). Fast crossovers on US500 or NAS100 are therefore the worst case, not the best.

The **turn-of-the-month** rule buys the S&P 500 at the close one day before month-end (some papers use four days) and sells at the close of the third trading day. It earned **7.2% a year with 6.9% volatility, Sharpe 1.04 and a −20.79% drawdown over 1926–2005**, costs not reported ([Quantpedia TOM](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/turn-of-the-month-in-equity-indexes)). It is contested. The Atlanta Fed found it disappeared after 1990 in S&P futures ([Atlanta Fed](https://www.atlantafed.org/research/publications/wp/2000/11.aspx)). An earlier note claimed a 2026 Quantpedia study showed the effect narrowing to day +1. The primary page shows that study is about **sector momentum** in the middle of the month, gross of costs, with Sharpe about 0.55. It is not an update of the index turn-of-month effect ([Quantpedia 2026](https://quantpedia.com/sectoral-intramonth-momentum-cycle/)).

Short-term dip-buying has practitioner support only. The Connors RSI(2) rule goes long when the close is above its 200-day average and the 2-day RSI is below 10, and exits when the close moves above the 5-day average. On SPY over 2010–2022 it showed a 7.57% annual return, a 62% win rate and a 23.5% maximum drawdown, with a slump in 2018–2021 ([QuantConnect](https://quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/15754/an-overview-of-a-simple-rsi-based-trading-strategy)). IBS is where the close sits in the day's range, (close − low)/(high − low). Buying when IBS is below 0.2 earned about 13% a year on SPY **before costs** ([Kinlay summary](https://jonathankinlay.com/?p=2199)).

Intraday momentum on SPY has the strongest reported net figure. Zarattini, Aziz and Barbon's "noise area" breakout traded 1-minute bars from 2007 to April 2024. It reports 19.6% a year and Sharpe 1.33 after commissions and slippage. Those numbers are secondary, and one co-author runs a trading-education firm ([SFI](https://www.sfi.ch/de/publications/n-24-97-beat-the-market-an-effective-intraday-momentum-strategy-for-s-p500-etf-spy)). Some edges are gone. The pre-FOMC drift "essentially disappeared" after 2015 ([Kurov et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finlet/v40y2021ics1544612320315956.html)). The large-cap pre-holiday premium was not significant over 1983–2019 ([Ko & Yang](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/CFR-0111)). Real-time volatility targeting of a single index generally fails to beat buy-and-hold on Sharpe ([Cederburg et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:138:y:2020:i:1:p:95-117)).

The dollar is the weakest asset in this review. One study, with an out-of-sample period of 1999–2020, found that technical rules' predictive power for G11 currencies "has completely disappeared" ([ADU repository](https://repository.adu.ac.ae/bitstreams/30d15095-739e-4967-a33f-7233bf0644df/download)). The Bouchaud-group data shows FX trend vanished alongside index trend ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.01550)). A 2003–2025 walk-forward student project found **EURUSD 12-month-style momentum reached only a 0.54 Sharpe** ([QuantInsti](https://www.quantinsti.com/articles/trend-following-strategies-major-currency-markets-epat-project/)). If you test the dollar at all, test slow monthly momentum on EURUSD and expect little.

## Retail signals, indicators and grid bots show no independent evidence of edge

The base rate matters most here. When ESMA restricted CFDs in 2018, national regulators had found that **74–89% of retail CFD accounts lose money, with average losses of EUR 1,600 to 29,000 per client**. The restrictions included leverage caps of 30:1 on major FX pairs and 20:1 on gold and major indices ([ESMA](https://www.esma.europa.eu/node/84933)). Almost all retail signal and bot products run on these accounts.

No peer-reviewed, independent audit of paid Telegram, Discord or app "AI" signal groups was found. The "verified ROI" rankings that circulate come from sites with no stated method that sell placements ([Flexe.io](https://flexe.io/blog/telegram-free-crypto-signals-performance-data/)). What academics have documented is the dark side. Xu and Livshits traced **220 pump-and-dump schemes organised in Telegram channels** in four months of 2018: about 100 channels running about two pumps a day, mostly in small coins ([USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity19/presentation/xu-jiahua)). The UK FCA charged nine people, including reality-TV influencers, in May 2024 over an unauthorised FX scheme promoted on social media ([AJ Bell](https://www.ajbell.co.uk/group/news/tv-stars-among-nine-finfluencers-charged-fca)). An FCA official noted that promoters get paid whether or not followers make money ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/britons-warned-to-watch-out-for-instagram-fx-scams/)). Copy trading shows the same pattern. The only positive eToro result came from an eToro-promoted 2012 study ([PRNewswire](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/tuning-social-networks-improves-financial-performance-on-etoro-social-investment-network-150723145.html)). An analysis of more than 25 million trades found copiers chasing riskier trades ([Bond University](https://bond.edu.au/news/fomo-wrecking-your-portfolio)), and experiments found that copy trading leads to excessive risk-taking ([BSE](https://bse.eu/research/publications/copy-trading)).

Popular TradingView scripts (Supertrend, UT Bot, LuxAlgo-style signals) have no independent out-of-sample, after-cost evidence. Supertrend's performance swings heavily with its ATR settings ([arXiv 2405.14262](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14262v1)). A self-published test found about a **42% win rate, negative results on 5-minute charts and weak results on daily charts** ([Liberated Stocktrader](https://www.liberatedstocktrader.com/supertrend-indicator/)). Most of these scripts are moving-average or ATR trailing-stop trend rules in new packaging. The data-snooping results above apply to them in full, and vendors who test thousands of settings and show the best one are exactly the case where a reported Sharpe is inflated.

Martingale bots double the position after each loss, and averaging-down bots add to a losing position. Both convert many small wins into a rare total loss. In one practitioner model, a five-step martingale ladder on a $2,000 buffer with a 55% win rate is **more likely than not to blow up within 38 sequences**, versus about 1.81% lifetime ruin for fixed $100 risk ([ThorTradeCopier](https://thortradecopier.com/blog/does-martingale-work)). One simulation puts the chance of ruin near 95% after 1,000 trades ([Autochartist](https://trader.autochartist.com/?p=68349)). Fixed-lot grids have bounded but trend-exposed losses ([Titan FX](https://research.titanfx.com/column/what-is-martingale-strategy)). MQL5 developers note that strong gold trends break such systems despite smooth backtests ([MQL5](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/767654)). A high win rate means nothing on its own. Expected profit equals win rate × average win minus loss rate × average loss, so a 95% win rate loses money if the rare loss is 20 times the typical win. The notes contain no study of plain fixed-schedule dollar-cost averaging (buying a set amount on a calendar). The criticism applies to bots that add size to losing positions with leverage.

## A 2025-onward backtest can screen rules but cannot prove them

The 2025-01-01 window is short, and gold's path through it was extreme. GLD rose about **235% from November 2022 to January 2026, then fell about 30% over the next five months** ([SentimenTrader](https://sentimentrader.com/blog/its-time-for-aggressive-traders-to-revisit-gld)). A trend filter will lag in the first leg and can only prove its worth in the second, so report the two legs separately. As a rough rule of thumb from the meta-research, a strategy's t-statistic is about its Sharpe ratio times the square root of the number of years. Reaching the t = 3 bar therefore takes about 9 years at Sharpe 1 and about 36 years at Sharpe 0.5. Under two years of data cannot validate anything, and monthly 24–48-month rules will make few or no trades in it. That is why the shortlist below uses **published settings exactly as written**, with no re-optimisation on 2025+ data. Every variant tried should be logged so the multiple-testing penalty can be applied ([CXO/Bailey et al.](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/insidiousness-of-overfitting-investment-strategies-via-iterative-backtests)). Costs should be stressed at 1.5 to 2 times your broker's actual spread, commission and overnight swap. The Forecast-to-Fill gold rule already reaches Sharpe zero at 1.5 times costs, so this stress test decides the outcome, not just the margin. Where possible, validate on longer futures history, such as COMEX gold and NYMEX crude back to the 1990s, before trusting a 2025+ result.

| Priority | Rule to backtest first | Data needed | Why it is on the list |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gold 1 | Monthly: hold if the 24-month return is above 0, or if price is above its 36/48-month average; run the 10-month average and 200-day average as faster checks | Daily or monthly closes | Only rule family that came close to passing data-snooping tests |
| Gold 2 | Forecast-to-Fill exits (−2×ATR14 stop, −1.5×ATR14 trailing stop, 30-day timeout) with 50-day momentum entry | Daily OHLC | Fully specified exits; serves as a cost stress test |
| Gold 3 | Overnight-only and Asian-session-only long versus their opposites | Hourly | Documented historically, weakened; check whether it survives your spread |
| Oil 1 | 12-month (and 1/3/12 blend) volatility-scaled trend, monthly | Daily closes plus swap | Commodity trend showed no post-2009 decay |
| Oil 2 | First-half-hour to last-half-hour momentum, keyed to actual EIA release times | 30-minute or hourly | Best oil-specific published pattern |
| Oil 3 | Long the day after an OPEC production-cut announcement | Event calendar | Small sample; treat as exploratory |
| BTC 1 | Zarattini Donchian ensemble, applied to BTC and ETH | Daily OHLC | Only after-fee result in the review |
| BTC 2 | Close above the 50-day average (20/100 as checks) | Daily closes | Peer-reviewed out-of-sample predictability |
| BTC 3 | MAX-20 next-day hold; 21:00–23:00 UTC long on high-volatility days | Daily / hourly | Held out of sample (MAX) or long-run (seasonality), gross only |
| Indices | 10-month average filter; turn-of-month (−1 to +3); RSI(2) below 10 with a 200-day filter | Daily | Slow or calendar rules; fast trend is dead here |
| Dollar | 12-month momentum on EURUSD | Monthly | Low expectations (Sharpe about 0.5 at best) |
| Controls | September/November gold, 5-minute MA crossovers, MIN buy-the-low on bitcoin | — | Expected to fail; a sanity check on your engine |

## Conclusion

Across all three priority assets, the evidence agrees on one point. What survives is slow trend following, and its value is mostly **avoiding the big drawdowns**, not beating a rising market. That changes the question to ask of a 2025+ backtest. It should not be "which rule made the most money?" but "did the rule step aside during gold's 2026 slide, oil's reversals and bitcoin's drawdowns, at my real costs?". The second surprise is how often the headline numbers fell apart on inspection. The 2.88-Sharpe gold paper fails its own cost test. Quantpedia's bitcoin rules disagree with their own strategy pages and report no costs. A claimed index turn-of-month update turned out to be a different study. That argues for reading primary tables before trusting any figure, including the ones repeated here.

The outlook also differs by market. The microstructure explanation for the decay of fast trend points to markets where fine price increments let high-frequency market makers absorb the trend. If that explanation is right, commodities such as gold and oil should keep rewarding trend rules longer than stock indices and currencies. Bitcoin's after-fee trend results rest on a single author team and need independent replication. Retail signal and bot products sit at the opposite end: they offer no evidence at all, and they make money from subscriptions, rebates and trading volume whether or not their users do.
